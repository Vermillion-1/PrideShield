Multimodal ML · Content Moderation

PrideShield

Memes defeat text-only moderation: the image is often benign, the caption is often benign, and the hostility exists only in their combination. PrideShield fuses CLIP visual and text embeddings into an MLP classifier, benchmarks three vision encoders under identical conditions, and evaluates against a text-only transformer baseline — on a corpus built and labeled by hand, with no budget.

Archived research code, developed July 2024 – May 2025 as an undergraduate major project at JUIT.

The Problem

Automated moderation is strong on text and weak on multimodal content. Anti-LGBTQ+ harassment in meme form exploits exactly that gap: meaning is carried by the interaction between image and caption, so unimodal classifiers systematically under-detect it.

The testable question. Does adding the visual modality measurably improve detection over a strong text-only baseline on this class of content?

The constraint that shaped everything

No funding for paid APIs, annotation services, or compute. That single constraint determined the architecture more than any modeling decision.

WantedBlocked byDone instead
Twitter/X collectionPaid-only after the 2023 API changesX dropped as a source
Bulk image-search APIsNo free tier at useful volumeSelenium browser automation
Commercial annotationCost per labelHand-labeled by two people
GPU computeNo grantColab free tier → frozen encoders, small head

The constraint was not purely limiting: frozen embeddings made the pipeline fast and reproducible. The real cost was to dataset scale, which bounds every result here.

Results

Best configuration — CLIP ViT-L/14@336px → MLP head, selected by a 30-trial Optuna study (AdamW, lr 0.01, weight decay 0, dropout 0.4, LeakyReLU, Focal Loss, layers [512, 256, 128]):

Read accuracy against the floor, not zero. The dataset is 80.9% positive, so a constant predictor scores 80.9%. The achievement is +13 points over trivial — and by the same measure RN50x64 at 84.85% is only ~4 points above a constant predictor. ROC-AUC (0.903) is the most robust single number here.

Two numbers worth pausing on. A median caption of 25 words sits comfortably inside CLIP's 77-token limit, but the 1,622-word maximum is why chunking was needed at all — the workaround exists for a small tail, not the typical case. And a mean edit distance of 8.5 characters between raw and corrected OCR quantifies how much repair the text actually needed.

Collection: six methods, three failed

The failures explain the architecture better than the successes.

MethodOutcome
Twitter/X via twint✕Broken by the 2023 API changes; official API paid-only. X dropped entirely — a real gap, since X is arguably where the most meme-format hate circulates.
ScraperAPI (commercial)✕Never funded. The prototype still contains scraper_api_key = 'YOUR API KEY' — the clearest artifact of the budget constraint.
requests + BeautifulSoup✕Image search results are JS-rendered; an HTTP fetch returns a shell page with no image URLs. This failure is what forced browser automation.
Reddit — PRAW / asyncpraw✓Free tier, rate-limited. An async rewrite overlapped requests to raise throughput within the same quota.
4chan — public JSON API✓No authentication required. Highest hostile-content density of any source, and the easiest to collect from — the two facts are related.
Selenium → DDG / Bing / Google✓Drives a real browser, waits for render, scrolls to trigger lazy loading, scrapes the live DOM. The most maintenance-heavy component in the pipeline.

Why deduplication was a correctness requirement, not housekeeping. The same viral memes appear on every platform, so multi-source collection returns heavy overlap. Duplicates landing on both sides of a train/test split leak the test set — the model scores well by memorizing, and reported accuracy becomes fiction. Perceptual hashing (imagededup) catches re-encoded, cropped and watermarked variants that exact hashing misses.

OCR was the largest quality bottleneck

Meme text is stylized, low-contrast, spread across panels with no reading order, and often rendered as part of the image rather than overlaid. EasyOCR handled the common cases and failed on the rest — dropped characters, merged words, spurious characters from background texture, scrambled reading order.

Why this mattered more than most architectural choices. The caption embedding is half the classifier input, and CLIP embeds nonsense confidently. Garbled OCR does not add noise — it produces a confidently wrong text embedding that poisons the fused representation.

Two correction stages were layered on top. A T5 sequence-to-sequence corrector handles structural damage — merged words, lost spacing — that spans token boundaries and so cannot be fixed by token-level spellchecking. Then Levenshtein/spellchecker repair fixes residual character-level errors. The edit-distance stage doubles as partial defense against the deliberate misspellings used to evade keyword filters.

Modeling

Design constraint: frozen encoders

Free-tier Colab could not fine-tune a 307M-parameter vision transformer, fixing the architecture early: frozen CLIP encoders as feature extractors, plus a small trainable MLP head. A compute decision before a modeling one — but embeddings compute once and cache, after which every experiment trains in seconds. That is what made a three-encoder comparison and a 30-trial search affordable at all.

How CLIP produces the embeddings

CLIP is pretrained contrastively on image–caption pairs: a vision encoder and a text encoder are trained jointly so that matching pairs land close together in a shared embedding space and mismatched pairs land far apart. That shared space is what makes it usable here — an image vector and a text vector are directly comparable, and both carry semantic content rather than raw pixels or tokens.

Class imbalance

At 80.9%/19.1%, an untreated model reaches ~80.9% by always predicting the majority class. Two strategies were implemented — Focal Loss, written from scratch ((1 − p_t)^γ down-weighting of easy examples), and class-weighted CrossEntropy.

Neither was assumed correct. The loss function was made a search parameter. Optuna selected Focal Loss for ViT-L/14@336px but class-weighted CrossEntropy for ViT-B/32 — the right imbalance strategy depended on the encoder, which fixing the loss in advance would have concealed.

What Worked, What Didn't

Finding: more parameters did not mean better representations

RN50x64 has the most parameters (~336M) and the widest embedding (1024-d) — and finished last, ~5 points below a ViT with roughly a quarter of its parameters.

Capacity was not the binding factor; representation quality was. A plausible reading: the task requires relating overlaid text semantics to image semantics, and ViT's global self-attention captures that relational structure in a way convolutional inductive bias does not. CLIP's ViT and ResNet variants also differ in training dynamics beyond architecture, so this is an interpretation rather than a proven mechanism.

Rejected variants

AttemptOutcomeWhy
Transformer over tabular featuresNo gain~924 examples cannot support the capacity; a concatenated embedding has no sequential structure for attention to exploit
MLP + BatchNormNo consistent improvementSmall batches on a small dataset make BatchNorm statistics noisy
MLP + L2 + early stoppingNo improvementDropout at 0.4 was already doing the regularization work
Deeper MLP headsOverfitDataset size

The consistent theme: every attempt to add capacity failed, and the binding constraint was always dataset size — which locates the problem in the data, not the architecture.

The fusion limitation

Concatenating two frozen embeddings is early fusion in its crudest form. The MLP sees two independent vectors glued together and must infer their relationship from scratch, with no mechanism for the modalities to attend to each other. For memes this is a real handicap: hostility lives in the interaction between image and caption — a benign image paired with a benign phrase producing a hateful whole. Cross-attention was out of reach on free-tier compute.

Future Work

This is 2024-era multimodal tooling. Frozen CLIP plus a head was defensible then; it is not what anyone should build now — and the parts that moved fastest are precisely the parts this project struggled with: multimodal fusion, OCR on stylized text, and labeling throughput.

Where the state of the art is

FamilyRelevance
Qwen2.5-VL / Qwen3-VLStrong open-weight VLMs, notably good OCR and text-in-image handling
InternVLCompetitive open-weight multimodal reasoning, strong on fine visual detail
LLaVA / LLaVA-NeXTReference open architecture for visual instruction tuning
Frontier APIsStrongest zero-shot multimodal reasoning, no training required

The relevant capability is not better image classification — it is that these models read the text inside the image and reason about its relationship to the imagery in one pass. That is exactly the operation concatenated CLIP embeddings cannot perform.

The rebuild, in order

  1. Establish the zero-shot baseline first. Measure a modern VLM zero-shot on the existing 1,320 examples, reporting macro metrics and AUPRC. This may end the project — if zero-shot matches 93.94%, the trained pipeline has no justification. This inverts the original workflow: collect→train→evaluate becomes evaluate-what-exists→collect-only-where-it-fails.
  2. Break the labeling bottleneck. VLM pre-labeling with human adjudication of low-confidence cases could cover an order of magnitude more data for the same effort. Keep contested items as soft labels rather than dropping them.
  3. Replace the fusion mechanism. Fine-tune a VLM directly (LoRA/QLoRA) so the model attends jointly over image and caption natively — and drop the OCR correction stack entirely, since modern VLMs read meme text far better than 2024-era OCR.
  4. Fix the evaluation protocol. A like-for-like text-only ablation on the same corpus; k-fold CV with confidence intervals; macro metrics and AUPRC as headline figures.
  5. Evaluate what gates deployment. False-positive rate on reclaimed in-group language — the dominant failure mode, and where errors do the most harm.
  6. Explanation, not just classification. A VLM can be prompted to explain why a meme is hateful, producing an auditable rationale — far more useful for moderation than a scalar.

What carries forward regardless of tooling: deduplicate before splitting; benchmark encoders under identical downstream conditions; treat the loss function as a search parameter rather than an assumption; and quote accuracy against the majority-class floor.

Notes & Caveats

Scope. An undergraduate project on free-tier compute, using a self-collected and self-labeled corpus of ~1,320 examples. The results hold within that scope.

Reading the metrics. Accuracy is best read against the 80.9% majority-class floor. Precision, recall and F1 are positive-class figures; macro averages are lower. ROC-AUC (0.9030) is the most robust single number here.

The multimodal comparison. The text baseline was trained on a public corpus (FRENK-LGBT) rather than on meme captions, so the ~7-point gap reflects both the added modality and the different training data — indicative, not a controlled ablation.

Test set. ~198 examples on a single fixed split. The encoder ranking is clear; the smaller gaps between them are not statistically established.

Dataset composition. 80.9% positive, English-only, drawn from a narrow set of platforms and a short time window. Items where the two labelers disagreed were dropped, so the final set skews toward clearer-cut examples.

Out of scope. Fairness and subgroup analysis, adversarial robustness, calibration, and false-positive rate on reclaimed in-group language were not evaluated — the last matters most for any deployed version. Ablations of the OCR-correction stack and the fusion strategy were also not run.

Dataset availability. The corpus is not publicly available. Release was not authorized by the supervising faculty, and publishing scraped hate speech targeting a marginalized community would be the wrong thing to do regardless.

If you build on this work: treat the classifier as a triage aid, not an adjudicator. False positives on reclaimed in-group language are the dominant failure mode for this class of model, and silencing the community the system exists to protect is a worse error than missing a slur.
